## Safety strip — the two tiers

<a id="safety"></a>

### Tier 2 — go now: call 911 or go to the ER

- **Heavy bleeding** — soaking a pad in an hour or less, clots, or gray/white tissue
- **Severe pain, one-sided pain, or pain with a shoulder-tip ache**
- **Fainting or near-fainting**
- **Fever of 100.4°F / 38°C or higher with any other symptom**
- **Can't keep fluids down for 24 hours, or can't pee normally**
- **Chest pain, trouble breathing at rest, or coughing blood**
- **Thoughts of harming yourself or the pregnancy** → call or text **988**, your OB, or go to the ER

### Tier 1 — call your clinic today

- **Any spotting or bleeding** — light, brown, once, or ongoing
- **New or different cramping; moderate pain**
- **Foul-smelling discharge**, especially after a procedure
- **A fever that stays up for 24 hours**, any level
- **Food won't stay down, but fluids will**
- **A calf that is suddenly warm, swollen, or painful on one side**
- **Anxiety you can't work, eat, or sleep through**; scary intrusive thoughts

> **Uncertainty always counts as a reason to call.** You will not be "the dramatic one." Clinics take this call a hundred times a week.

## 2. IVF Medical Protocol Tracker

<a id="protocol"></a>

### Why you are still on hormones

After an IVF transfer — especially a frozen transfer or a suppressed cycle — your body may not make enough progesterone on its own. Medication bridges the gap until the **luteo-placental shift**, usually between **weeks 8 and 12**. Clinics legitimately differ on when to stop. **Stopping is your RE's decision, not a checklist item.**

### Progesterone and estrogen

| Medication | Common forms | Why it matters | Typical stop window |
|---|---|---|---|
| Progesterone in oil (PIO) | IM injection, upper-outer glute, often 50–100 mg daily | Keeps the uterine lining stable | ~8–12 weeks; many clinics stop at 10 |
| Vaginal progesterone | Suppository, gel, or tablet; once or twice daily | Same goal, different route; less systemic effect | ~8–12 weeks |
| Estradiol | Oral 2 mg once or twice daily, patch, or vaginal | Supports the lining in FET/donor protocols | ~8–12 weeks, often with progesterone |

**Taper vs. hard stop:** some clinics halve the dose or go every other day for 1–2 weeks; others stop outright. Both are used. **Never create your own taper.**

**After a stop:** mild cramping or light spotting can happen from the withdrawal. It is a known effect — and you still report any bleeding.

<details>
<summary><strong>Injection-site care (PIO)</strong></summary>

- Upper-outer glute, alternating left and right.
- Warm compress 5–10 minutes before; gentle massage after.
- Lumps, soreness, and firm nodules are common — the oil is irritating. They can take weeks to resolve.
- **Call the clinic** for spreading redness, warmth, or fever.
- Never stop PIO because a site hurts. Call instead.
- Ask your clinic about numbing, site rotation, or switching part of your support to vaginal form if sites are badly knotted.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Refill buffer</strong></summary>

Keep **at least 7 days** of every medication on hand. Order 7–10 days before you run out. PIO is often a compounding special order — pharmacies may need lead time.

| Days on hand | What to do |
|---|---|
| 14+ | Fine. Next reminder 7–10 days out. |
| 7–13 | Order when convenient this week. |
| Under 7 | Order today. |
| 0 | Call the pharmacy and the clinic. Never stretch a hormone supply by skipping. |

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Travel, sharps, and other prescriptions</strong></summary>

- Carry medications in carry-on luggage, in original labeled boxes, with a copy of the prescription.
- PIO can thicken in the cold — let it come to room temperature before injecting.
- Use an FDA-cleared sharps container, or a heavy plastic container with a lid when traveling. Never loose household trash.
- Antibiotics, antifungals, opioids, and many other prescriptions can interact with IVF support medications. Tell every prescriber you are on them. `[VERIFY LOCALLY with your clinic's interaction list]`

</details>

### Missed-dose flow

<a id="missed-dose"></a>

**Step 1 — What did you miss?** `PIO` · `vaginal progesterone` · `estradiol` · `aspirin` · `prenatal` · `other`

**Step 2 — Roughly how long ago?** `under 2 h` · `2–8 h` · `8–24 h` · `more than a day`

#### If it was PIO — any gap

> **A missed PIO dose is a clinic call, not a guessing game.**
>
> Your clinic will either say **skip-and-resume** or **take it late**. Never take a double dose. Never stop cold turkey.

What the call will likely cover:

1. **Skip the missed dose** and take tomorrow's on schedule, or
2. **Take today's dose late** and keep the normal schedule after.

Which one they choose depends on their protocol and how late it is. You do not need to decide in advance. Have your schedule open and read them the timing.

☐ Call the main line · ☐ Call the after-hours line · ☐ Copy a note for the call

**Copy-paste note:**

> "I'm `____w ____d` post-transfer. I take PIO `____ mg` IM daily at `________`. I missed the dose on `________`. It has been about `____` hours. What do you want me to do — skip-and-resume or take it late? I will not double up without your say-so."

#### If it was vaginal progesterone, estradiol, aspirin, or a prenatal

- **Under 2–8 hours:** take it now, then keep the regular rhythm.
- **Same day, later:** it is fine to take it late. Today is still "the day." Never take two to catch up.
- **Any progesterone or estrogen gap of more than 24 hours:** **call before restarting.** Do not restart with a double. The clinic sets the resume plan.
- **Aspirin or prenatal, more than a day:** skip the missed one and resume at the next scheduled time. Mention it at your next visit.

> **Never two doses to make up one. Never stop a hormone cold turkey.**
>
> If you think you accidentally took a double dose, call your clinic now. If you feel unwell, call 911.

### Appointment timeline

| When | Appointment | What it is for | Window |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9–11 days post-transfer | **Beta hCG #1** | A first quantitative hCG | Clinic-specific |
| 48 hours later | **Beta hCG #2** | The trend — the actual unit of information | 48 h |
| Every 48–72 h as needed | **Serial betas** | Until ultrasound takes over | Clinic-specific |
| 6w0d–6w6d | **First ultrasound** | Sac, yolk sac, fetal pole, cardiac activity | Some clinics 6.5–7w |
| 7–14 days later, if needed | **Repeat scan** | Follow-up for an indeterminate finding | Not a verdict |
| 8–10 weeks (range 8–12) | **Graduation to OB** | Records handoff and medication plan | Ask your RE |
| From 10 weeks | **NIPT / cfDNA** | Screens T21, T18, T13, sex chromosomes | One blood draw |
| 11w0d–13w6d | **NT scan** | Nuchal translucency + combined screen | CRL 45–84 mm |
| 18–22 weeks | **Anatomy scan** | Full structural check; confirms "boy" | Usually 20w |

<details>
<summary><strong>Beta hCG #1 — what it can and cannot tell you</strong></summary>

- **What happens:** one blood draw.
- **What it can tell you:** whether hCG is present and roughly how it compares with your clinic's starting threshold (many use about 50–100 mIU/mL as encouraging).
- **What it cannot tell you:** whether the pregnancy is in the right place, or how it will progress. One number is not the story. A strong beta does not rule out an ectopic; a low first beta does not doom anything.
- **Ask:** "Will you draw again, and when? When do we stop betas and switch to ultrasound?"

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Beta hCG #2 — what the trend means</strong></summary>

hCG roughly doubles every 48–72 hours very early, then naturally slows.

| Starting level | Well-validated minimum rise in 48 h |
|---|---|
| Low | about **53%** |
| Under 1,500 mIU/mL | about **49%** |
| 1,500–3,000 mIU/mL | about **40%** |
| Above 3,000 mIU/mL | about **33%** |

Past roughly 6,000–10,000 mIU/mL, doubling time stops being meaningful — ultrasound takes over. About 1% of viable pregnancies rise more slowly than the 53% rule. A slower rise is a **"we follow up"** signal, not a verdict.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>First ultrasound (6w0d–6w6d) — including "no heartbeat yet"</strong></summary>

They look for, in this order: gestational sac → yolk sac → fetal pole → cardiac activity. All four rarely appear at once at exactly 6w0d.

**What it can tell you:** location (inside vs. outside the uterus), measurable growth, and whether a heartbeat is visible *yet*.

**What it cannot tell you:** a final answer at 6w0d. The thresholds are deliberately conservative — built so a healthy pregnancy is never accidentally declared failed.

- **Diagnostic of early loss:** CRL ≥7 mm with no cardiac activity; or mean sac diameter ≥25 mm with no embryo.
- **Suspicious, not diagnostic:** anything smaller and quiet → repeat scan in **7–14 days**.
- **Indeterminate findings are followed, never declared.**

**If you get an indeterminate result:** see the [waiting plan](#waiting-plan). A healthy embryo grows about **1 mm per day** in this window — 7–14 days is enormous measurable progress.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Graduation to the OB (8–10 weeks, range 8–12)</strong></summary>

- Your RE closes out; your OB takes over.
- Records transfer, a last scan if scheduled, the medication taper plan, and your dating.
- Ask: **"What week do I graduate, and what gets sent to the OB?"**
- The "abandoned" feeling is real and common. Your RE may stay reachable for questions — ask directly.
- Bring the [My Protocol](#my-protocol) table to the handoff.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>NIPT / cfDNA (from 10 weeks)</strong></summary>

- **What it is:** one blood draw. The lab reads placental DNA fragments in your blood — not fetal blood.
- **What it screens:** trisomy 21 (>99% detection), trisomy 18 (~98%), trisomy 13 (up to ~99% in some analyses), plus optional sex chromosomes and microdeletions. It is a **screen, not a diagnosis**.
- **Boy result:** a "male" call means Y-chromosome DNA was detected. With an adequate fetal fraction, it is about **98–99% accurate**.
- **Caveats:** a low fetal fraction can produce a **no-call** (about 1–5% of first draws; most redraws succeed). A vanishing twin, a prior transplant, or a transfusion can also shift results.
- **Confirmation:** the **18–22-week anatomy scan** is the visual confirmation and the full structural check.

**If NIPT comes back no-call:** it is not bad news. It means "not enough sample from that draw." Ask your clinic about a redraw. Do not self-interpret a low fetal fraction.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>NT scan (11w0d–13w6d) and the anatomy scan (18–22 weeks)</strong></summary>

**NT scan:** a ultrasound measuring the fluid layer behind the fetal neck. It requires a CRL of 45–84 mm to be valid, and is often paired with a blood screen (PAPP-A and free beta-hCG). It is a screening test, not a diagnosis. A "borderline" result leads to a conversation, not a conclusion.

**Anatomy scan:** 30–45 minutes. Checks organs, growth, placenta position, and — for a boy — visibly identifies penis and scrotum. Testes will be described as inside the abdomen; that is normal at this stage and descent happens in the third trimester. A small hydrocele is common and usually resolves. Occasionally a detail needs a second look; re-checking is routine.

</details>

>

> **You logged moderate cramping — a same-day clinic call fits.**

### If you have no symptoms at all

> **Noted. Feeling quiet is allowed.** Around 70–80% of pregnant people get nausea, which leaves 20–30% who don't — healthy pregnancies land on both sides. If a long-standing symptom suddenly disappears, that is the one version of quiet worth a call.

### Waiting plan for a repeat or indeterminate scan

<a id="waiting-plan"></a>

The 7–14 days between an indeterminate scan and the repeat is the highest-anxiety interval of early pregnancy. Decide three rules once, today:

1. **A 15-minute daily worry window** at a set time. Outside it, postponed worries wait.
2. **One real distraction block per day** — a walk, a show, one errand.
3. **One human to text instead of Googling.** A nurse line counts.

Write the worst-case fear down **once**, read it **once**, done. A pre-decided plan beats "we'll figure it out if it's bad." Unknowns are where scanxiety lives.

**What the follow-up will look for:** a cardiac flicker, growth between the two scans (a healthy CRL grows about 1 mm/day), and a yolk sac that behaves. Seven to fourteen days is enormous measurable progress in this window.

### Red flags — the full list

**Tier 2:** heavy bleeding (pad soaked in ≤1 hour), clots or tissue, severe/unilateral pain, shoulder-tip pain, fainting, fever ≥100.4°F with other symptoms, can't keep fluids down 24 h, chest pain/SOB, calf swelling/pain, thoughts of self-harm.

**Tier 1:** any spotting, new/different cramps, moderate pain, foul discharge, fever lasting 24 h, food won't stay down, anxiety interfering with function.

### Worry journal

Prompts:

- Worst-case, written once.
- What am I afraid of?
- What is the plan if it happens — even a bad plan calms this.

> **Worry window:** set 15 minutes a day. Outside the window, postponed worries wait for the next one. The window is the plan — not suppression, just scheduling.

### Grounding

**Box breathing:** In for 4. Hold 4. Out for 4. Hold 4. Four rounds.

**5-4-3-2-1:** Name 5 things you can see. 4 you can touch. 3 you can hear. 2 you can smell. 1 you can taste.

**At 2 a.m.:** open Safety (one tap), text your scan partner, write the worst case once, and pick tomorrow's worry-window slot. Apps do not replace people; they shrink the search space.

> **If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or the pregnancy:** that is a medical emergency, not a character flaw. Call or text **988**, your OB, or go to the ER. Help exists during pregnancy, not just after.

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 5 — Poppy seed · ~2 mm</strong></summary>

The neural tube is closing; the heart is starting as a simple tube. Your embryo is genetically XY already — but at this size, there is no visible difference between boy and girl. Nothing to see; everything to build.

**Gentle focus:** rest is productive this week.
**Not visible yet:** nothing is scannable yet — first scans run from 6w0d.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 6 — Lentil · ~4 mm</strong></summary>

The heart starts beating — most often visible around 6w0d–6w3d on an early scan. Arm and leg buds appear. The gonad is still "bi-potential" at this point, able to go either way.

**Boy note:** none visible — the Y chromosome is working, but the hardware hasn't differentiated.
**Gentle focus:** book the scan date; that's the whole job.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 7 — Blueberry · ~1 cm</strong></summary>

The brain is growing fast; arm and leg paddles now.

**Boy note:** the milestone week. The **SRY gene** on the Y chromosome activates in the gonad during weeks 6–7 and points it toward becoming a testis. Sertoli cells begin forming.
**Gentle focus:** the first 12 weeks are the organ-building window.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 8 — Raspberry · ~1.6 cm</strong></summary>

Fingers and toes are beginning; movement starts (not felt yet by you).

**Boy note:** the testis starts making its first testosterone — low, then ramping.
**Gentle focus:** profound fatigue is normal and hormone-driven. Naps are a clinical intervention, not a weakness.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 9 — Grape · ~2.3 cm</strong></summary>

Essential organs have all begun forming. The tail is regressing.

**Boy note:** testosterone is climbing toward its first-trimester peak (weeks ~8–12).
**Gentle focus:** the "why am I still on shots" feeling peaks around here. The taper question is a week-8-to-12 conversation, and the RE decides per your protocol.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 10 — Kumquat · ~3.1 cm</strong></summary>

Officially a fetus at the end of this week. Fingernails appear; kidneys begin making urine.

**Boy note:** testosterone peaks around weeks 8–12 and drives the Wolffian ducts into epididymis, vas deferens, and seminal vesicles; DHT shapes external anatomy.
**Gentle focus:** the NIPT window opens. Ask the clinic which panel they use.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 11 — Fig · ~4.1 cm</strong></summary>

Head is about half the body — normal. Hearing structures are developing.

**Boy note:** testosterone peaks then begins falling back; DHT turns the genital tubercle toward a penis and fuses the folds into a scrotum.
**Gentle focus:** the progesterone-taper emails from other apps are not your protocol. Yours is in weeks 10–12 territory, per your RE.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 12 — Lime · ~5.4 cm</strong></summary>

Liver and kidneys are functioning; the risk of miscarriage drops meaningfully once a heartbeat is confirmed and keeps falling with each week.

**Boy note + caveat:** external genitalia are largely formed by ~12–13 weeks — but **you cannot read sex from an ultrasound this week.** Not at any accurate level, and especially not via a "nub" guess. NIPT is where "boy" comes from right now; the 18–22-week anatomy scan is the confirmation.
**Gentle focus:** if relatives ask for a "12-week gender scan," hand them NIPT's reliability instead.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 13 — Peach · ~7.4 cm (CRL)</strong></summary>

Vocal cords are forming; the intestines finish moving back into the abdomen.

**Gentle focus:** end of trimester one — tally it up honestly: what got you through, who helped, what to keep.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 14 — Lemon · ~8.7 cm head-to-heel</strong></summary>

Squinting, frowning, making grasping motions; facial muscles are working. Measurements switch from crown–rump to head-to-heel from here.

**Boy note:** sex-differentiated external genitalia are usually visually identifiable on scan from ~14 weeks onward — but seeing it isn't the purpose of any scan you'll get.
**Gentle focus:** first-trimester growth is remarkably steady (gestational age in days ≈ crown–rump length in mm + 42). Known-stability facts help on edgy days.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 15 — Apple · ~10.1 cm</strong></summary>

Light comes through closed eyelids; the skeleton hardens from cartilage to bone.

**Gentle focus:** normal bodily changes (aching back, growing appetite, round ligament twinges) start here — all standard.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 16 — Avocado · ~11.6 cm</strong></summary>

Hearing is developing (muffled); first movements may be felt soon — usually 17–20 weeks for first pregnancies.

**Gentle focus:** book a telehealth or a brief check-in if a scan is still far ahead and quiet days are heavy.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 17 — Pear · ~13 cm</strong></summary>

Cartilage keeps turning to bone; practicing "breathing" amniotic fluid.

**Gentle focus:** second-trimester energy usually returns — spend it deliberately.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 18 — Bell pepper · ~14.2 cm</strong></summary>

Yawning, hiccuping; ears reach their anatomical position. The anatomy-scan window opens (18–22 weeks).

**Boy note:** on the scan, you'll likely see penis and scrotum identified; the testes will be described as inside the abdomen — completely normal at this stage; descent completes in the third trimester, sometimes even after birth.
**Gentle focus:** ask whether your clinic will do the anatomy scan at 18 or 20 — both are in-window.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 19 — Mango · ~15.3 cm</strong></summary>

Vernix (a waxy coating) is starting; hearing is sharper.

**Gentle focus:** a support person at the anatomy scan changes the experience — plan it now.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 20 — Banana · ~16.4 cm</strong></summary>

Halfway point. The anatomy scan often happens here.

**Boy note:** the standard second-trimester reading for a boy: visible male genitalia, intra-abdominal testes, occasionally some scrotal fluid (hydrocele) — all expected and usually self-resolving before or shortly after birth.
**Gentle focus:** if anything on the scan needs a second look, that's the norm for anatomy scans — thousands get re-checked without drama.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 21 — Carrot · ~26.7 cm</strong></summary>

Swallowing more amniotic fluid; rapid eye movements begin.

**Gentle focus:** sleep on either side is fine — left-side optimization is a third-trimester thing.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Week 22 — Papaya · ~27.8 cm</strong></summary>

Looks like a tiny newborn proportionally; sense of touch is well developed. Viability outside the womb becomes *possible* from ~22–24 weeks with intensive NICU support — a biologic fact, not a promise.

**Gentle focus:** if your clinic says you're past 22 weeks, your OB is leading now.

</details>

### Milestones worth marking

| Moment | What to say to yourself |
|---|---|
| Transfer day | "Transferred." A day-5 blast, on schedule. This is the starting point a healthy pregnancy is meant to begin from. |
| First beta | "First number in." From here, the trend does the talking — exactly how your clinic is watching. |
| First heartbeat | "First heartbeat, on screen." This is the moment ultrasound takes over from betas — intended, not lucky. |
| Graduation | "You graduated." Your RE handed you to your OB with your notes. The lead role changes; the relationship doesn't have to end. |
| Testosterone ramp (8w) | "A quietly boy-specific week." Hormones are doing sex-differentiation work. The baby never notices. |
| Anatomy scan | "Confirmed." Both a structural check and the visual confirmation of what NIPT pointed at. |

## Learn / FAQ

<details>
<summary><strong>1. Is spotting normal after IVF?</strong></summary>

About 20–30% of pregnancies have some first-trimester bleeding. IVF adds implantation and medication-related spotting to the mix. Most bleeding does not mean the pregnancy is failing. But the clinic wants a call for **any** spotting — light or brown — every time. That rule is not scaremongering: it is how clinics catch the serious causes early while routinely reassuring on the common ones. Soaking a pad in an hour, passing clots or tissue, or feeling dizzy → the ER list, now, not tomorrow.

**Call your clinic if…** you see any blood at all — the "harmless-looking" kind included.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>2. Are mild cramps normal?</strong></summary>

Mild, on-and-off, both-sided, period-like cramping is common from the uterus stretching. Severe pain, one-sided pain, or pain with a shoulder-tip ache is not — that pattern is the ER list. Moderate-but-not-one-sided pain gets a same-day call. Dizziness or fainting with any of it → ER. Nothing here is about how much you can "tolerate" — it is a plain pattern map.

**Call your clinic if…** the cramps are new or different, moderately bad, or you just can't tell. Uncertainty counts.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>3. What if I have no symptoms at all?</strong></summary>

Symptoms do not read the pregnancy. Around 70–80% of pregnant people get nausea, which leaves 20–30% who don't — and healthy pregnancies land on both sides. Absence of nausea is not a sign of loss. The one asymmetry worth knowing: a long-established symptom *suddenly going silent* is worth a phone call, not an ER trip. Either way, don't track "am I still nauseous?" as a health metric.

**Call your clinic if…** an established symptom vanished abruptly and you can't set it down.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>4. When do I stop progesterone?</strong></summary>

Most clinics stop or taper between 8–12 weeks, once the placenta has taken over. Some taper (alternate days, or half dose, then stop); some stop outright; vaginal progesterone sometimes runs a bit longer than PIO. There are legitimate differences and high-risk exceptions that go longer. Stopping is always the RE's decision — timed to *your* protocol, never a do-it-yourself taper. After a stop, light cramping or spotting can occur from withdrawal — expected, and still always report any bleeding.

**Call your clinic if…** you're within a week of your set stop date and haven't confirmed the plan.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>5. What does a beta number actually mean?</strong></summary>

hCG roughly doubles every 48–72 hours very early on, then the pace naturally slows. Minimum expected rise in 48 hours depends on the starting level: about 53% when low, ~49% under 1,500, ~40% from 1,500–3,000, ~33% above 3,000. Past ~6,000–10,000, doubling time stops being meaningful and ultrasound takes over. A single number cannot distinguish inside from outside the uterus, and a great single beta does not rule anything out — the trend is the unit of information.

**Call your clinic if…** a result confuses you. Reading it out loud to a nurse is cheaper than Googling it.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>6. When is my first scan, and what if there's no heartbeat yet?</strong></summary>

Typically 6w0d–6w6d, sometimes later. They look for the sac, yolk sac, fetal pole, and cardiac activity — in that order. The thresholds are deliberately conservative: a CRL of ≥7 mm with no heartbeat, or a mean sac of ≥25 mm with no embryo, are diagnostic; anything *smaller*-and-quiet is "suspicious," which means a rescan in 7–14 days — that is the designed plan, not limbo. The thresholds were set specifically so a healthy pregnancy is never mistakenly called a failure.

**Call your clinic if…** the appointment was "indeterminate" and no follow-up scan got scheduled.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>7. Is it definitely a boy? When will I see it?</strong></summary>

NIPT looks for Y-chromosome DNA fragments from the placenta. From 10 weeks with adequate fetal fraction, the "male" call is right roughly 98–99% of the time — excellent, but still a screen, not a certainty. First-trimester ultrasound cannot reliably distinguish genitalia, and a "12-week nub" guess isn't a method. The 18–22-week anatomy scan is the visual confirmation, and it is the same scan that checks all the organs.

**Call your clinic if…** you want to know what a specific NIPT phrasing on your report means.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>8. What does the anatomy scan check for a boy?</strong></summary>

Two things: normal anatomy everywhere, and — confirming what NIPT said — penis and scrotum, visibly. Two boy-specific findings that sound alarming and aren't: testes inside the abdomen at 18–22 weeks (they normally descend in the third trimester, sometimes even after birth), and a little scrotal fluid (hydrocele), which usually resolves on its own before or shortly after birth. Your sonographer may note either; both are in-normal-range findings.

**Call your clinic if…** the report wording confuses you. The exact phrasing matters, and your OB reads it in context.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>9. How does the RE-to-OB handoff work — and why do I feel dumped?</strong></summary>

Graduation is typically 8–10 (sometimes 8–12) weeks, once the heartbeat is confirmed, often on two scans. It is a records handoff: a last scan, the medication taper plan, your dating. Your RE doesn't disappear — many stay reachable; ask. The "abandoned" feeling after graduation is a real, common thing among pregnancy-after-IVF patients. It has nothing to do with the quality of either clinic; it is the end of a very supported period, and the new support structure hasn't warmed up yet.

**Call your clinic if…** graduation is close and nobody has told you what week it is or what gets sent.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>10. Do I need pelvic rest, and until when?</strong></summary>

Pelvic rest means nothing in the vagina — no intercourse, no tampons — and for many protocols no orgasm at all (contractions are the concern, not just penetration). It is **not** universally required after IVF. Clinics prescribe it variously: none after the transfer week, 1–2 weeks until a heartbeat, or "until bleeding resolves." **If you are on vaginal progesterone, do not stop it because of pelvic rest** — ask your RE exactly how to give it during pelvic rest. The only honest source is your RE. Bladder pressure, non-sexual daily activity, and household stress are not pelvic-rest issues.

**Call your clinic if…** you're not sure whether you're on it. Ask, don't guess in either direction.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>11. What can't I eat or drink? What's my caffeine limit?</strong></summary>

**Avoid:** unpasteurized anything and soft cheeses from unpasteurized milk; deli/cold cuts and hot dogs unless reheated to steaming; refrigerated pâté and smoked fish unless cooked into a dish; raw sprouts even home-grown; runny eggs and raw dough; raw/rare meat; liver and high-dose vitamin A skincare/supplements; shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish, bigeye tuna, marlin, orange roughy; all herbal supplements before clearing them; alcohol — zero is the safe amount. Limit albacore tuna to ~6 oz a week; best fish picks (2–3 servings/week): salmon, sardines, shrimp, tilapia, pollock, catfish, light tuna. **Caffeine: ≤200 mg/day** — roughly one 12-oz coffee; watch tea, cola, chocolate, matcha, energy drinks.

**Call your clinic if…** you're mid-consumption and worried. Realistically, that is a next-visit question, not an emergency.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>12. Is my anxiety "too much," and what do I do at 2 a.m.?</strong></summary>

Roughly 1 in 5 pregnant women experience clinically significant anxiety, and IVF patients start higher. It isn't a character flaw; it is a physiologically loaded condition with real treatment options — CBT-based therapy has the best evidence, and medication is a genuine option for many. Panic, inability to function, or scary intrusive thoughts are medical events, not personality notes. The 988 lifeline serves pregnant people too. At 2 a.m.: open Safety (one tap), text your scan partner, write the worst case once, and pick tomorrow's worry-window slot.

**Call your clinic if…** you're unable to work, eat, or sleep because of it. That is a same-day conversation, not a white-knuckle one.

</details>
