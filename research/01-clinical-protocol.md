# 1. IVF-Specific Medication Management (Weeks 4–12)

## Why you're still on hormones
After an IVF transfer — especially for a frozen embryo transfer (FET) or a cycle with ovarian suppression — your corpus luteum may not make enough progesterone on its own the way a spontaneous pregnancy would. Medications "bridge the gap" until the **luteo-placental shift**, when the placenta takes over hormone production, typically between **weeks 8 and 12** (most commonly effective by ~10 weeks). Clinical trials comparing stopping at various points after the luteo-placental shift have found similar ongoing-pregnancy and live-birth rates, so clinics differ on exactly when to taper. **Never stop on your own** — stopping is a decision your RE makes, timing it to your specific protocol.

## Progesterone — the most important medication to take consistently

| Form | Typical route | Typical regimen (yours may differ) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Progesterone in oil (PIO) | IM injection, usually gluteal (upper-outer quadrant) or sometimes ventrogluteal | 1 mL (e.g., 50–100 mg) daily, "as prescribed by your RE" | Gold standard for luteal support for decades; oily sesame or castor oil vehicle |
| Vaginal progesterone (suppository, gel, or tablet; e.g., 100–600 mg equivalent) | Vaginal, once to twice daily | Often used alone or alongside PIO | Fewer systemic side-effects; can cause discharge; gel leaves no mess beyond applicator |
| Intramuscular 17-hydroxyprogesterone caproate | Weekly IM | Only in specialized indications (e.g., prior preterm birth — not routine IVF support) | Distinct drug from PIO; do not confuse |

**Why continuation matters:** progesterone keeps the uterine lining stable and supports implantation and early embryo development. Abrupt discontinuation in the first trimester, before the placenta has taken over, is not something to experiment with. (Miscarriage after IVF is most often due to embryo chromosomal abnormalities, *not* to how support is given — so don't let medication-dosing anxiety compound scanxiety.)

**Typical stop/taper windows (legitimately varies):**
- Most common: stop or taper between **~8–12 weeks** of gestational age, i.e., roughly 3 weeks to 7 weeks after a 5-day transfer. Many clinics stop PIO at **10 weeks**; many continue to end of week 12.
- Some clinics taper: e.g., every other day doses for 1–2 weeks, then stop; others halve the dose; others stop outright. Vaginal progesterone is sometimes continued a bit longer than PIO or vice versa.
- In some high-risk situations (e.g., recurrent loss protocols, documented luteal deficiency), clinics extend support longer.
- After stop, mild cramping or light spotting can occur from the withdrawal — that's expected and your clinic can reassure you, but always report bleeding anyway (Section 4).

**Injection-site care (PIO):**
- Inject into the upper-outer gluteal quadrant ("sister to sister" positioning helps reach it). Rotate sites. Warm compress for 5–10 minutes before, and gentle massage after, improves comfort and absorption.
- Lumps, induration, and soreness are common (the oil is irritating); some solid nodules (granulomas) form and slowly resolve over weeks to months. Watch for infection signs: spreading redness, warmth, fever — then call the clinic.
- Consider asking your clinic about mixing agents they endorse, alternating glutes, cold pack before/numbing, or switching part of your support to vaginal form if sites are badly knotted.
- Never stop PIO just because a site hurts — call the clinic instead.

## Estrogen (for FET/donor protocols)
- Oral estradiol (e.g., 2 mg tablets once to twice daily), transdermal patches, or vaginal estrogen may be part of a FET or donor-egg endometrial protocol.
- Taper windows are clinic-specific — commonly tapered/stopped somewhere between ~ weeks 8–12 alongside progesterone. Some clinics stop estrogen earlier at first good ultrasound; others run it to 12 weeks. **Per your protocol; confirm with your RE.**
- Note: you can be on PIO and still benefit from a FET protocol that includes no estrogen at all — protocols differ.

## Other medications you may see (general rationale only)
| Medication | Why it may be prescribed | Reminder |
|---|---|---|
| Low-dose ("baby") aspirin 81 mg | Evidence for preeclampsia prevention in selected higher-risk pregnancies; some clinics use it for implantation factors or prior loss | Never start/stop based on the internet. If added for preeclampsia risk, it's typically started between 12–28 weeks — not necessarily now. Per your RE/OB. |
| Enoxaparin (Lovenox) / heparin | Anticoagulation for histories of clotting disorders, prior poor outcomes, or specific protocols | Very specific to your history; specialist-managed. |
| Prednisone (low dose) | Sometimes used in select IVF protocols for immunologic reasons; controversial and not routine | Usually tapered in the first trimester; never stop abruptly — steroids need weaning. |
| Levothyroxine | Hypothyroidism management; thyroid levels monitored in pregnancy (target TSH often <2.5 in early pregnancy per RE preference) | Dose often goes up ~20–30% in pregnancy — dose set by labs, not guesswork. |
| Metformin | PCOS, insulin resistance, sometimes gestational diabetes prevention | Often continued into pregnancy; per your prescriber. |
| Prenatal vitamin with ≥400 mcg (0.4 mg) folic acid | Neural-tube and general risk reduction; some protocols use 1 mg+ or 4 mg (e.g., prior NTD or after weight-loss surgery) | Look for USP-verified; take with food if nauseous. |

## Missed or late doses — generic, safe rules
- **Same-day realization:** take it as soon as remembered, that day. **Never take two doses to "make up" for a missed one.**
- **PIO is the exception to self-managing:** a missed IM dose is important — the safest generic rule is to skip and do the remaining day's dose on schedule OR take late, per what the clinic says. **Call your clinic's after-hours line for any missed PIO dose** rather than guessing.
- **Never stop a hormone medication cold turkey** in the first trimester without clinic guidance. If you realize you've been off progesterone >24 h, call — don't restart a double dose on your own.
- Antibiotics, antifungals, opioids, and many other medications can interact with your regimen — tell every prescriber you are on IVF support meds. [VERIFY LOCALLY with clinic's list]

## Refill, travel, and sharps logistics
- **Refill buffer:** keep ≥7 days of every medication on hand; set refill reminders 7–10 days before running out. PIO is an injectable that pharmacies (even specialty compounding pharmacies) may need lead time to produce — order early.
- **Travel:** carry medications in carry-on luggage, in original labeled boxes; carry a copy of prescriptions; a doctor's note helps for injectables/syringes at security. PIO in oil can thicken in cold — let it come to room temperature before injecting. Never leave hormones in a hot car.
- **Sharps disposal:** use an FDA-cleared sharps container or, if traveling, a heavy-plastic container with lid (e.g., laundry detergent bottle) — never the household trash loose. Many pharmacies, hospitals, and police/fire stations have free sharps take-back; check your county page. [VERIFY LOCALLY: your local drop-off rules.]

# 2. Monitoring Milestones & Schedule

## A. Serial beta-hCG

| Timing (5-day transfer anchor) | What happens |
|---|---|
| 9–11 days post transfer (most clinics day 9–11; some do day 7/8 preliminary) | First quantitative beta-hCG. Many clinics have a threshold (e.g., ~50–100 mIU/mL) that predicts doing well; values vary widely at this point, so **one number alone tells much less than the trend.** |
| 48 hours after the first (commonly at ~11–14 dpt) | Repeat beta. Rule of thumb: at least **~53% rise in 48 h** when the initial level is low, rising from a values-dependent minimum (see below). |
| Every 48–72 h (sometimes 2–3 more draws) | Until the value rises above the discriminatory zone and an ultrasound can take over. Some clinics stop betas at ~1,500–2,000; most stop serial betas once a viable intrauterine pregnancy (sac ± yolk sac with heartbeat) is confirmed on scan. |

**What the numbers mean (and don't):**
- hCG generally **doubles every ~48–72 h in very early pregnancy**, then the rate naturally slows. **Well-validated minimum expected rise in 48 h:** roughly **53% at low levels**, and slower at higher starting levels: about **49% below 1,500**, **40% between 1,500–3,000**, and **33% above 3,000 mIU/mL** — values above ~6,000–10,000 make doubling time largely meaningless, which is why clinics switch to ultrasound.
- A single hCG level **cannot** reliably distinguish an intrauterine from an ectopic pregnancy. A "great-looking" beta does not rule out ectopic, and an initially slow rise does not doom the pregnancy. Some (~1%) viable pregnancies rise less than 53%/48 h.
- **Discriminatory zone:** above about **3,500 mIU/mL (ACOG's threshold)** a gestational sac should be visible on transvaginal ultrasound. (Some institutions use 1,500–2,000.) If levels are above the zone with no sac seen, your clinic will rule out ectopic and other possibilities. Clinics vary on which threshold they use; your own clinic's number governs.
- **One fairly reliable sign** for reassuring normal singleton IUP trend: a pattern of slowing growth after ~6,000–10,000 is normal, not a red flag, once ultrasound has confirmed viability.

**Ectopic warning signs:** see Section 4. IVF does not eliminate ectopic risk — IVF pregnancies can, if anything, have a slightly elevated ectopic rate. A beta trend can't confirm it either way; only ultrasound (and sometimes surgery) can.

## B. First ultrasound — timing and what is seen

**Typical first scan: 6w0d–6w6d (sometimes 6.5–7w, or when hCG crosses the discriminatory zone).**

What's typically visible (things appear in roughly this order):
- **Gestational sac** (from ~4.5–5 wks; usually visible from hCG of ~1,500–3,500)
- **Yolk sac** (from ~5.5 wks)
- **Embryo, first as a "fetal pole,"** (from ~6.0 wks; measurement = crown–rump length, CRL)
- **Cardiac activity** (flicker visible once embryo ~2–3 mm / roughly 6–6.5 wks)

**Viability criteria used by sonographers (from SRU/ACOG/NICE alignment):**
- **Diagnostic of early pregnancy loss:** CRL **≥7 mm** with **no** cardiac activity; or mean sac diameter (MSD) **≥25 mm** with no embryo; or no cardiac activity ≥11 days after sac+yolk sac first seen; or ≥2 weeks after sac alone.
- **Suspicious but NOT diagnostic** — requires a repeat scan, typically **7–14 days later**: CRL <7 mm without heartbeat; MSD 16–24 mm without embryo; small sac relative to embryo (MSD–CRL <5 mm); slow or borderline heart rate for the stage; yolk sac >7 mm; empty amnion sign.
- This means a 6-week scan often **cannot give a final answer** — and that okay-ness is a promise, not a lapsed standard: thresholds are deliberately conservative (100% specificity) so a healthy pregnancy is never mistakenly declared failed. **Indeterminate findings are followed, never declared.** Ask your clinic when the follow-up scan is; typical is 1 week.

## C. Graduation to OB
- Typically at **8–10 weeks**, sometimes **8–12**, when the heartbeat is confirmed, especially on two scans, and medications are tapered. Some high-risk or pregnant-after-loss patients stay a little longer with their RE. Ask your RE for your graduation date and what the OB will get in the records. It's a wonderful milestone.

## D. First-trimester combined screening (optional, per OB)
- **Nuchal translucency (NT) ultrasound:** performed **11w0d–13w6d**, requires **CRL 45–84 mm**. Measures a thin fluid layer behind the fetal neck; increased NT is associated with chromosomal differences and some structural issues. Must be performed by a certified provider (e.g., FMF-credentialed), and the OB often pairs it with the first-trimester blood screen (PAPP-A and free beta-hCG) at the same visit to produce a combined risk figure.

## E. NIPT / cell-free DNA (cfDNA)
- **Timing:** valid from **10 weeks** of gestation (a minority of labs down to 9 weeks), can be drawn any time after that through term.
- **Mechanism:** it reads **placental DNA fragments** (not fetal blood) in your circulation — so it reflects the placenta's chromosomal makeup, which is usually identical to the fetus's, rarely not.
- **Screens (as a screen, not a diagnostic):** trisomy 21 (>99% detection), trisomy 18 (~98%), trisomy 13 (~99% detection in some meta-analyses), plus optional sex chromosome aneuploidy (45,X / 47,XXX / 47,XXY / 47,XYY) and, at some labs, optional microdeletion panels. **No single screening test is diagnostic; only CVS (~10–13 wks) or amniocentesis (~15+ wks) are.**
- **Sex chromosome / fetal sex results caveats:**
  - Requires adequate **fetal fraction** (usually ≥4%); low fetal fraction → "no-call" / redraw or re-test later (this also carries an independent signal worth discussing, but a single low fraction is common in early draws or with higher BMI).
  - Vanishing twin (even a resorbed second sac from your transfer or a spontaneous twin) can throw off both sex and aneuploidy results.
  - Prior organ transplant from a male donor, recent blood transfusion, certain maternal mosaicism, maternal sex-chromosome anomalies, or some cancers can produce misleading results.
  - If your NIPT is a **"male" call, it means Y-chromosome DNA was detected.** A "female" call is only the absence of Y reads, which is slightly less certain than a positive Y call.
  - **Ultrasound at 18–22 weeks remains the confirmatory anatomic check** — for sex as well as for structural anatomy.
- **No-call / redraw:** ~1–5% of samples don't yield a result the first time; most redraws succeed. Don't panic; ask your clinic what the next step is.

## F. Male (XY)-specific testing notes
- **How NIPT determines sex:** the test looks for **Y-chromosome fragments** among the cell-free DNA. Presence of Y reads → male call. Accuracy after 10 weeks with adequate fetal fraction is roughly **98–99%** — excellent, but a screen, not a certainty.
- **Why you still get the anatomy scan:** the 18–22 week ultrasound both confirms what NIPT said about sex and, more importantly, checks anatomy that NIPT never looks at. For a boy, the sonographer will usually identify:
  - **Penis and scrotum** — the standard "boy" identification on second-trimester scan.
  - **Undescended testes are normal at this stage** — testes first travel down the abdomen into the pelvis early in development, and reach the scrotum typically in the **third trimester** (commonly by ~28 weeks but can be later, even after birth in some). "Testes not in scrotum" on a second-trimester scan is **expected**, not a finding.
  - **Hydrocele / scrotal fluid** is commonly seen on second-trimester scans and usually resolves on its own before or shortly after birth; it's a benign, self-limiting finding. It can occasionally be associated with something downstream, so the sonographer may note it and follow-up is rarely needed. [VERIFY LOCALLY if flagged on your scan.]
- **Important regardless of sex:** external genitalia **cannot be reliably distinguished on first-trimester ultrasound** (roughly until ~14 weeks for a skilled eye, and never a good idea to rely on a "12-week nub" guess made by non-professionals). An NIPT male result is much more reliable than a first-trimester "gender guess" ultrasound.

## G. Optional screening and avoidance considerations (confirm with your RE/OB)
- **Carrier screening:** expanded carrier panels (often including CF, SMA, Fragile X and more) are typically offered pre-conception or in early pregnancy regardless of sex of fetus. If not previously done, it can be drawn any time; results are most useful early.
- **Cystic fibrosis and SMA screening** are the classic universal screens in the US, regardless of ethnicity, and are often done off the same blood draw as your other early labs. [VERIFY LOCALLY — coverage and lab options vary.]
- **Glucose (diabetes) screening:** typically **24–28 weeks** (late). Not a first-trimester test unless you have risk factors (PCOS, prior gestational diabetes, elevated HbA1c, BMI above threshold, strong family history). If you have PCOS+metformin history, your OB may order an **early** glucose or HbA1c — that's a personalized call, not the default. [VERIFY LOCALLY per your OB.]
- **Infection-avoidance targets:** see Section 3, Food safety and Toxoplasmosis/CMV.

## H. Dating and EDD recalculation for IVF
- **Day-5 blastocyst transfer:** EDD = **transfer date + 261 days** (equivalently, transfer date + 37 weeks + 2 days). The retrieval date is "2w0d" and the transfer is "2w5d."
- Because IVF dating is known exactly, **your clinic's IVF date usually beats an LMP-based or app-based estimate**. If a first-trimester ultrasound CRL measure disagrees with the IVF date by more than a few days, your OB will typically reconcile — but transvaginal ultrasound CRL does carry ±5–7 days of measurement error, so small "disagreements" are measurement noise, not a problem. **Note for your dashboard: display the day-5-transfer EDD as the primary and the "gestational age = days past transfer ÷ 7 + 2 weeks" as the timer.**

# 3. Safety Guidelines

## Foods — what to avoid and why
| Risk | Avoid / limit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Mercury** (neurologic hazard) | **Avoid:** shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish (from the Gulf of Mexico), bigeye tuna, marlin, orange roughy. **Limit albacore ("white") tuna to ~6 oz/week** (one serving). **Best Choices (2–3 servings/week):** salmon, sardines, shrimp, tilapia, pollock, catfish, light (skipjack) tuna, cod, canned light tuna. | FDA/EPA joint advisory. Fish are a good brain-building omega-3 source — the advice is "choose low-mercury fish," not "avoid all fish." |
| **Listeria monocytogenes** (miscarriage, stillbirth, neonatal sepsis) | Unpasteurized milk and **all soft cheeses made from unpasteurized milk** (brie, camembert, feta unless labeled pasteurized, queso fresco, blue cheese); **deli/cold cuts and hot dogs** unless reheated until steaming; **refrigerated pâté, meat spreads, smoked fish (e.g., lox)** unless it's in a cooked dish; unwashed pre-made deli salads; **unpasteurized juice and cider**. | Listeria survives cold, unlike most bacteria. When in doubt, heat it until steaming. |
| **Salmonella** | Raw or undercooked eggs, runny yolks in freshly made dishes, raw cookie dough/cake batter (unless eggs are pasteurized), very-rare or raw meat and poultry, homemade Caesar/hollandaise/aioli. | Cook eggs until whites and yolks are firm; cook meat to safe internal temps. |
| **Toxoplasmosis** | **Cat litter** (have someone else change it; if you must, wear gloves and wash hands), **gardening without gloves**, unwashed produce, raw/undercooked meat (especially pork, lamb, venison), washing cutting boards between raw meat and ready-to-eat items. | Toxoplasma gondii. Pregnant women were *not* told to give up their cat — indoor cats previously exposed pose low risk. Note that indoor-only cats less often carry the parasite. |
| **CMV (cytomegalovirus)** **especially if you have toddlers at home** | Saliva and urine of young children — don't share utensils/cups/food with toddlers, avoid kisses on the mouth, wash hands after diaper changes/wiping noses/bath time. | CMV is the leading infectious cause of congenital hearing loss and neurologic issues; common in toddler settings. |
| **Vitamin A (retinol)** | Avoid **liver/liver pâté**, and any supplement containing high-dose retinol; avoid **isotretinoin/Accutane** and typical retinoid skincare creams (topical absorption is small but the "abstain" recommendation is easiest — ask your RE/OB; tretinoin topicals are usually stopped in pregnancy). | High doses cause birth defects.**Note:** beta-carotene from plants (carrots, sweet potato) is completely safe. |
| **Alcohol** | **None.** No known safe amount in pregnancy. | Alcohol causes fetal alcohol spectrum disorder; the safest amount is zero. |
| **Caffeine** | **≤200 mg/day** (roughly one 12-oz coffee; watch tea, cola, chocolate, matcha, energy drinks which can be 80–300 mg). | ACOG guidance; higher intake associated with miscarriage/low birth weight risk in some studies, though data isn't perfect. |
| **Herbal supplements** | Avoid all herbal teas, supplements, and "natural" remedies before clearing them with your RE/OB. | Supplements aren't FDA-reviewed for safety in pregnancy; several (e.g., high-dose chasteberry, blue cohosh, high doses of various botanicals) have concerning signals. |
| **Raw sprouts** | Alfalfa, clover, radish, mung bean sprouts — even home-grown. | Bacteria that live inside the seed cannot be washed away. |

## Pelvic rest — what it actually means, and who actually needs it
- **Definition:** **nothing in the vagina** — no intercourse, no tampons, no manual stimulation, and (many protocols) **no orgasm at all** (douching applies too). Uterine contractions caused by orgasm are the concern, not just penetration.
- **Critical IVF exception:** many patients are on **vaginal progesterone** at the same time. Do not stop a prescribed vaginal suppository because of pelvic rest; ask the RE exactly how the medication should be given while on rest. This is a common point of confusion and a medication-safety issue.
- **Common post-IVF reasons for temporary pelvic rest:** fresh bleeding or a **subchorionic hematoma (SCH)**, a very recent transfer, a more cautious clinic protocol, a history that suggests higher risk, or occasionally just "until the heartbeat is confirmed."
- **Common durations** (legitimately varies): for a simple recent-transfer instruction this is often ~1–2 weeks after transfer until the first heartbeat, or first trimester-only; for bleeding/SCH it's typically **until bleeding resolves** or sometimes until the second trimester. Some clinics prescribe **no pelvic rest at all** in a routine, uncomplicated IVF pregnancy beyond a transfer week.
- **The key nuance:** pelvic rest is **NOT universally required** after IVF. Ask your RE explicitly, "Do I need pelvic rest, and if so, until when?" and record their exact answer. If you THINK you're on period rest and aren't sure — call and ask rather than assume either way.
- Bladder/bowel pressure, non-sexual daily activity, and household stress do not need to be managed as pelvic rest issues.

## Exercise, movement, and heat
- **General recommendation for uncomplicated pregnancy:** ~**150 minutes per week** of moderate-intensity activity (e.g., brisk walking, swimming, stationary bike, prenatal yoga, light weights), spread over the week. Day-by-day: some movement most days.
- **Avoid or modify:** contact sports and activities with a fall/abdominal-trauma risk (skiing, horseback riding, off-road biking); **scuba diving** (absolute avoid); **hot yoga / hot tubs / saunas / very hot baths** in the *first trimester* in particular (raising core temperature isn't recommended early); exercises with **breath holding/Valsalva** in the later trimesters; exercising flat on your back after ~20 weeks if lightheaded.
- **Modify if you have bleeding, SCH, cramping, or on pelvic rest:** your RE may tell you to back off to walking-only or rest more; adapt to their instruction, not a random app. If you're bleeding at all at any point — see Section 4.
- Motion sickness trade: exercise can help nausea and constipation; even 10 minutes of walking counts.

## Everyday exposures
| Exposure | Practical guidance |
|---|---|
| **Hot tubs / saunas** | Avoid early pregnancy; core temperature elevation is the concern. Cool (not hot) baths are fine; warm (not scalding) baths are generally OK. |
| **Hair dye / nail salon** | Most-researched guidance says standard coloring/highlighting and salon visits are acceptable; the small doses don't reach levels that matter. Ventilation helps; many prefer waiting until the second trimester as personal choice. Ask your OB if you want specifics. |
| **Dental care** | Do **go** — routine cleaning, x-rays with abdominal shielding, and most needed treatment is safe and recommended; untreated gum disease is itself a pregnancy risk factor. Mention you're pregnant. **Local anesthetic without epinephrine or with it is considered safe — don't skip care because you're pregnant.** |
| **Sex (if not on pelvic rest)** | Sexual activity isn't dangerous in uncomplicated pregnancy, but if your RE wants pelvic rest, that includes intercourse and orgasm. Nausea/fatigue/breast tenderness legitimately reduce desire — that's normal. Blue/violet spotting after sex is often harmless cervical contact bleeding, but always report it per Section 4 if you're in doubt. |
| **Sleep position** | **You do not need to sleep on your left side yet.** Left-side is a late-pregnancy optimization. In the first trimester, sleep however is comfortable; only avoid long periods flat on your back if you feel lightheaded, short of breath, or nauseous when you do. Once you're late-second/third trimester, either side is fine, and pillows help. |
| **Travel** | Generally safe in an uncomplicated pregnancy through most of the second trimester. **Confirm flight/travel limits with your RE in the first trimester** — some have instructions if there's bleeding, SCH, or a high-risk situation. Walk/stand on flights ≥4 hours, hydrate, wear a seatbelt low across hips if in a car, and check if your clinic has any specific advice about travel distances from your hospital. |
| **Vaccines** | **Flu (any trimester), COVID-19 (recommended in pregnancy per CDC/ACOG), Tdap at 27–36 weeks, RSV per latest season guidance.** **Avoid LIVE vaccines** (e.g., MMR, chickenpox/Varivax, live intranasal flu) during pregnancy. If you need a live vaccine, schedule it postpartum. Clear all travel vaccines with your provider. |
| **Work & occupational exposure** | Anesthesia gases, industrial solvents, high heat, heavy radiation, lead, pesticides — especially relevant if your work touches these; bring your safety data sheets (SDS) to your OB. If you're healthcare/laboratory staff, radiation-exposure rules from your workplace apply, and typical healthcare X-ray/dental environments (scattered dose) are below occupational pregnancy limits with standard protection. Ask both for a written clearance. |

# 4. Red Flags — When to Escalate

## The two-tier rule for the dashboard
- **Tier 1 —"Call your clinic" (during business hours; use after-hours line if clinic says):** light spotting, mild cramping, nausea/vomiting that's annoying but tolerable, medication side effects, low-grade symptoms, everything you're just not sure about. **Uncertainty is always a reason to call.**
- **Tier 2 —"Go to the ER / call 911 now":** (a) heavy bleeding, (b) severe or one-sided pain, (c) fever ≥100.4 °F/38 °C with any other symptom, (d) fainting or near-fainting, (e) can't keep any fluids down. Multiple symptoms at once → go straight to the ER. **Heavy bleeding + pain, or fainting, or fever with severe pain = ER, not waiting for a callback.**

## 1. Bleeding and what "too much" means
- **Some bleeding or spotting is common:** roughly **20–30% of pregnancies have some first-trimester bleeding**; IVF pregnancies can have implantation or medication-related spotting. Most first-trimester bleeding does **not** mean the pregnancy is failing — but at the same time, you should never self-reassure: **"light" or "brown" does not give permission to skip a call.**
- **Likely benign:** light pink or brown spotting, no clots, not filling a pad, stops within 1–3 days, no pain.
- **Call the clinic (Tier 1):** any spotting at all — including the "harmless" kind. (This is the calm rule, and it's the right one.)
- **Escalate (Tier 2):** heavy bleeding — **soaking a full pad in ≤1 hour**, passing **clots** (especially large ones or gray/white tissue), or bleeding with dizziness/racing heart.
- Remember PIO can cause hematomas at injection sites — but don't attribute vaginal bleeding to a shot site. Never assume bleeding is "just the progesterone" without the clinic.

## 2. Pain — what's typical and what isn't
- **Typical and common:** mild, on-and-off, both-sided, low cramps/stretchy "growth" type of discomfort in the first trimester (round ligament-type pulling appears more in late first/early second).
- **Call the clinic (Tier 1):** new or worsening cramping that feels different; moderate pain that isn't one-sided.
- **Escalate (Tier 2):** **severe or worsening pain**, especially if it is **one-sided** and/or with **shoulder-tip pain** (pain where your shoulder tip meets your back — this suggests internal bleeding irritating the diaphragm), **dizziness or fainting** — this is the classic **ectopic** picture and requires ER evaluation **right now**, not a morning-after phone call.
- IVF (particularly any history of tubal disease, prior ectopic, or a transfer into a tube-less uterus) does not exempt you; IVF pregnancies can still ectopically locate.

## 3. Fever / infection
- **Fever ≥100.4 °F (38 °C)** — escalate to the clinic at minimum; fever with chills, foul discharge, or significant vaginal bleeding is Tier 2.
- Fever persisting ≥24h at any level → call.
- **Foul-smelling vaginal discharge** — call the clinic, especially post-instrumentation (transfer, hysteroscopy).

## 4. Vomiting on the extreme side (hyperemesis)
- **Normal-ish:** morning nausea and vomiting that you can manage with food strategies, rest, and possibly Rx after clinic discussion.
- **Call the clinic (Tier 1):** you can't keep food down but you're keeping fluids down.
- **Escalate (Tier 2):** unable to keep fluids down **for 24 h**, **dry heaving with reduced urine output**, producing very dark or scant urine, feeling faint or confused, weight loss beyond ~5% body weight.
- Note: ketones and dehydration are the immediate concern, and IV steroids/IV fluids sometimes work far better than oral meds when you're at the dehydration stage. Never just push through.

## 5. Vascular (blood clot) warning signs — especially relevant if on Lovenox/heparin/estrogen-containing protocols
- **Chest pain, shortness of breath at rest, coughing blood, racing heart** → **Tier-2 ER now** (possible pulmonary embolism).
- **Calf pain, warmth, or swelling that's one-sided (especially if asymmetric)** → clinic same day, or ER if it's severe or with chest symptoms (possible DVT).
- **Severe new headache, changes in vision, or sudden swelling of face/hands** — individually they're more preeclampsia-signaling later in pregnancy, but in early pregnancy with a clinic background of hypertension or clotting history these still warrant a same-day call at minimum, and ER if severe. [VERIFY LOCALLY with your OB if you have a history of hypertension or clotting.]

## 6. Metabolic/hormonal emergencies (rare but worth knowing)
- Severe dizziness with sweating and confusion after fasting/diarrhea — especially if on any medication that can cause hypoglycemia — same-day call.
- Severe abdominal pain, swelling, or jaundice (yellow skin/eyes) — ER.

## 7. Sudden mental-health emergencies
- Thoughts of harming yourself, thoughts of hurting the baby, panic attacks that feel unmanageable, hallucinations — these are medical emergencies. **Call 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), your OB, or go to the ER.** Postpartum-focused help is also available during pregnancy.

# 5. Symptoms & Mental Health

## What's normal in weeks 4–12
| Symptom | Typical picture |
|---|---|
| **Nausea / vomiting** | Affects roughly **70–80%** of pregnant people; ranges from mild queasiness to daily vomiting. Starts ~week 5–6, peaks ~8–10, improves by 12–14 (though not always). Empty stomach makes it worse; small frequent bland meals, ginger, B6 (with RE/OB approval), and prescription options exist. |
| **Fatigue** | Profound — often the earliest and most relentless symptom. |
| **Breast changes** | Tenderness, heaviness, darkening areolas, blue veins — often one of the first and most persistent signs. |
| **Bloating / constipation** | Progesterone slows gut motility. Water, fiber, walking, and (with OB approval) safe stool softeners help. |
| **Food aversions** | Sudden disgust at formerly favorite foods, especially meat, coffee, strong smells — normal and often protective. |
| **Nasal congestion** | "Pregnancy rhinitis" — progesterone + increased blood volume. Saline spray, humidifier, safe allergy med per OB. |
| **Cramping** | Mild, diffuse, period-like — normal from uterine stretching. One-sided/severe = Tier rules above. |
| **Spotting** | Light pink/brown common; **always call** even if you think it's harmless. |
| **Increased vaginal discharge** | Thin, white, mild-smelling — normal (progesterone effect). Foul smell, itching, or color change → clinic. |
| **Round ligament pain** | More common in late first/second trimester — sharp, brief, side-pain with movement/sneezing. |
| **Frequent urination** | Often one of the earliest symptoms, persists through trimester one. |

## The progesterone confusion (and the equally important reverse)
- **Progesterone side effects can mimic pregnancy symptoms:** bloating, breast tenderness, mild nausea, fatigue, mood swings. So some symptoms out of "feeling terrible" may be partially medication — and that's fine.
- **The reverse is what matters more:** some women have almost no symptoms and carry healthy babies; some have horrific symptoms and miscarry. **Symptom strength is not a reliable signal of pregnancy health either way.** Absence of nausea is not a sign of loss; sudden *loss of long-established symptoms* is a data point worth a phone call, not an ER visit — always just call the clinic.
- **Do not track "am I still nauseous?" as a dashboard metric.** It creates anxiety and tells you nothing actionable. If you want a metric, track the schedule the clinic set.

## Mental health — this deserves real care, not platitudes

**Prevalence you should know:**
- About **1 in 5 (roughly 20%)** of pregnant women experience perinatal anxiety or depression at clinically significant levels; IVF/ART patients have higher baseline rates than spontaneous-pregnancy patients, given the stress, financial, and loss-risk experience you've been through. [VERIFY LOCALLY with a mental-health resource if you want citations.]
- **Validated screening tools your OB may or may not use:** the **GAD-7** (Generalized Anxiety Disorder, 7-item, also used in pregnancy), **EPDS** (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, standard at OB intake and postpartum), and the **PASS** (Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Scale). **If your OB's intake doesn't include a screen, you can self-administer the GAD-7 and bring the score** — it's a legitimate, validated instrument.

**Coping techniques that are actually evidence-informed (not just vibes):**
1. **Box breathing (4-4-4-4):** inhale 4, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4 — 2–3 minutes; downregulates the sympathetic nervous system.
2. **5-4-3-2-1 grounding:** name 5 things you see, 4 you can touch, 3 you hear, 2 you smell, 1 you taste — interrupt rumination spirals.
3. **Worry window:** schedule 15 minutes a day to worry *on purpose*; outside the window, postpone worries to the next window. Well-supported in anxiety therapy.
4. **Limit Googling / symptom-checking** — set an app limit; "prenatal anxiety Googling" is one of the biggest amplifiers. Ask a human (a nurse line) instead of an algorithm.
5. **Scheduled nurse check-ins:** ask your clinic if you can set a fixed cadence ("I'll call every Tuesday at 2pm regardless of whether I'm panicked")—this turns panic-driven calls into a schedule and reduces the "I can't call because it's silly" spiral.
6. **Peer support:** Resolve (National Infertility Association) support groups; IVF-specific pregnancy-after-loss groups; a therapist who does perinatal mental health (search Postpartum Support International's directory — they have a provider listing and a helpline).
7. **Therapy (CBT-based) has the best evidence** for perinatal anxiety; medication is safe for many in pregnancy — that's a personal risk/benefit conversation with your OB or psychiatrist, not a solo decision.

**When to seek help (Tier-1 clinic call at minimum, and often same-day mental health care):**
- Panic attacks (racing heart, chest tightness, feeling of impending doom, hyperventilation).
- You're unable to work, eat, sleep, or function because of anxiety.
- **Intrusive thoughts** — scary, unwanted thoughts (e.g., of harm coming to the baby, or of harm coming to yourself). These are more common than people admit and treatable, and shame is the thing that stops people from seeking help. Tell someone.

## "Scanxiety" — specific help for the pre-appointment window
- Name it; it's the most common term IVF patients use for the days before a scan or a beta result. It's normal after loss or after IVF transfers.
- **Rituals that help patients:** distraction blocks (workouts, errands, friends), a "scan partner" you text at specific times, written "worry script" (write the worst-case outcome you're afraid of once, read it once, done), a countdown you can't refresh, and a **pre-decided plan** for a bad outcome (which is far less anxiety-inducing than "we'll figure out if it's bad"). Asking your RE "what would we do if X" turns unknowns into a script, and unknowns are where scanxiety lives.
- **If a scan is at 8 AM and you are going to spiral for 24 h before, ask whether a telehealth or brief-pre-visit check is possible.** Some clinics will let you review the report in advance with a nurse — that doesn't always work, but it never hurts to ask.

# 6. Weekly Fetal Development (Boy-Specific, W5–22)

> **Sizing anchors:** Weeks 5–13 use **crown–rump length (CRL)** with fruit/veg stand-ins. From ~week 14 on, the fetus curls less, so length is measured **head-to-heel**. Weeks 5–13 measurements are standard embryology CRL values; pop-culture fruit sizes are approximations (they vary by fruit/person). **Explicit note: external genitalia are not reliably distinguishable on first-trimester ultrasound** — an NIPT result is the male-specific data source this early.

| GA | CRL (or head-to-heel) | Size analogy | Key development (with male-specific milestones) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **5** | ~2 mm | **Poppy seed** | Neural tube closing; heart begins as a simple tube. Genetically XY, but sex is invisible — no macroscopic difference yet between XX/XY embryos structurally at this stage. |
| **6** | ~4 mm | **Lentil** | Heart begins to beat (activity often seen ~6w0d–6w3d on TVUS). Arm/leg buds appear. **Gonadal ridge exists but is "bipotential" — still able to become either ovary or testis.** |
| **7** | ~1 cm | **Blueberry** | Brain rapidly enlarging; arm/leg paddles. **MILESTONE (male): the SRY gene on the Y chromosome activates in the indifferent gonad during week ~6–7, directing it toward a testis.** Sertoli cells begin forming. |
| **8** | ~1.6 cm | **Raspberry** | Fingers/toes beginning; movement begins (not yet felt). **(male): fetal testis begins steroidogenic function — testosterone production starts, low then ramping.** |
| **9** | ~2.3 cm | **Grape** | All essential organs have begun forming ("critical period" ends at ~10 wks). Tail regressing. **(male): testosterone rising toward its first-trimester peak.** |
| **10** | ~3.1 cm | **Kumquat** | Embryo → officially called a **fetus** at the end of week 10. Fingernails appear; kidneys begin making urine. **(male): testosterone peaks around weeks ~8–12; drives Wolffian-duct growth into epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles. DHT drives external male differentiation.** |
| **11** | ~4.1 cm | **Fig** | Head ~half the body (normal); hearing structures developing. **(male): testosterone peaks ~ wks 8–12 then begins to fall back; DHT from it converts the **genital tubercle** toward a penis and fuses labioscrotal folds into a scrotum.** |
| **12** | ~5.4 cm | **Lime** | Risk of miscarriage drops meaningfully after a heartbeat is confirmed and continues to fall with gestational age; liver/kidneys functioning. **(male): external genitalia clearly differentiating — the penis/scrotum shape is largely formed by ~12–13 wks, though still tiny.** |
| **13** | ~7.4 cm (some charts still CRL ~7.4 cm here or switch to head-heel) | **Peach** | Vocal cords forming; intestines finishing their move back into the abdomen. Transitional week in most charts — can be CRL or head-to-heel depending on the source. |
| **14** | ~8.7 cm, head-to-heel | **Lemon** | Squinting, frowning, making grasping motions; facial muscles working. **(male): sex-differentiated external genitalia are usually visually identifiable on ultrasound from ~14 wks onward (but not the reason for a scan).** |
| **15** | ~10.1 cm | **Apple** | Can sense light through closed eyelids; skeleton is hardening from cartilage. |
| **16** | ~11.6 cm | **Avocado** | Hearing developing (muffled sound); fetal movements you may soon feel (usually not until 17–20 for first-timers). |
| **17** | ~13 cm | **Pear** | Cartilage → bone continuing; can practice "breathing" amniotic fluid. |
| **18** | ~14.2 cm | **Bell pepper** | Yawning, hiccuping; ears in anatomical position. An anatomy scan window opens (~18–22 wks). **(male): the anatomy scan will usually identify penis and scrotum visibly. Testes are anatomically still inside the abdomen/pelvis — completely normal.** |
| **19** | ~15.3 cm | **Mango** | Vernix caseosa (waxy coating) starting; hearing sharper. |
| **20** | ~16.4 cm | **Banana** | Mid-pregnancy. Often when the anatomy scan happens. **(male): the standard second-trimester finding = "visible male genitalia, intra-abdominal testes" and possibly mild hydrocele/scrotal fluid — both normal at this stage.** |
| **21** | ~26.7 cm (head–heel), ~10.5 in | **Carrot** | Working on swallowing more amniotic fluid; rapid eye movements begin. Growth from here is tracked head-to-heel. |
| **22** | ~27.8 cm (~11 in) | **Papaya** | Looks like a tiny newborn proportionally; sense of touch well-developed. Viability outside the womb begins to be *possible* at ~22–24 wks with intensive NICU support — a biologic fact, not a promise. |

> **Dashboard note:** After week 13, most apps switch to head-to-heel length and measured weight. If you print these numbers, use head-to-heel from week 14 onward and CRL from week 5–13. Trimester 1 growth is remarkably linear (GA in days ≈ CRL in mm + 42) — a nice known-stability fact for any anxiety moment.

# 7. Clinic Variation & Personalization

## Where your RE may legitimately do things differently
| Decision point | Common variation across clinics | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Beta schedule** | First draw at 9, 10, or 11 days post 5-day transfer; 1, 2, or 3 total draws; some stop after hCG >1,500, others after a heartbeat. | "When will you draw, how many times, and when do you stop betas?" |
| **Progesterone stop / taper** | Anywhere from ~8–12 weeks; some stop at 8, some at 10, some at 12; some taper (every other day/half dose), some stop outright; some continue a FET protocol longer. | "What exact week do I stop, and is it a taper or a hard stop?" |
| **Estrogen taper** | Some FET protocols drop at first heartbeat; some continue to 10–12 weeks. | "Do I stay on estrogen, and what week does it stop?" |
| **Pelvic rest** | From nothing at all after transfer week, to 1–2 weeks, to first trimester, to until bleeding resolves. | "Do I need pelvic rest, and until exactly when?" |
| **Graduation timing** | 8–10 weeks (common); 8–12 weeks; earlier if OB insists; later for high risk/loss history. | "What week do I graduate, and what do you send to the OB?" |
| **First scan** | Some at 6w0d, some wait to 6w4d–7w to reduce indeterminate results. Ask why they picked their day — often just a known-good trade-off. | "When will I scan, and what happens if we can't see the heartbeat yet?" |
| **NT/NIPT timing** | NIPT drawn tail-end of first trimester vs. right at 10 wks; some offer NIPT to every patient, some based on age/insurance; some patients do NIPT first and skip combined screening, some do both, some opt for sequential. | "Which test, when, and do I need both NT and NIPT?" |
| **Aspirin / Lovenox / prednisone** | Added (or not) based on your history, not a default. Some clinics have particular preeclampsia/DVT start windows. | "Are any of these on my protocol, and when would they start/stop?" |
| **Repeat viability scans** | Some do 2 scans (6w, 8w); some fold a second in at 7–8 w; some go to a growth scan at 12. | "How many scans before graduation?" |
